# Deep Field Works — Content Strategy

## Purpose of this document

This document defines the content strategy for Deep Field Works.

It explains what the site publishes, why those artifacts exist, how they relate to one another, and how raw material should move from notes into public work.

This document is intended for:

* human planning
* ChatGPT project context
* Codex and agent workflows
* future publishing automation
* site architecture decisions

It should be used before drafting or publishing.

Use this document to decide **what should be made**.

Use `editorial-guidelines.md` to decide **how well it is written**.

---

# Core purpose of Deep Field Works

Deep Field Works is a public record of thinking about how humans, organizations, interfaces, and AI systems are changing together.

It is not a marketing site.

It is not an AI news site.

It is not a personal brand funnel.

It is not primarily designed to build an audience.

It is a working archive for:

* observations
* hypotheses
* models
* prototypes
* field reports
* essays
* conceptual frameworks
* checkpoints in evolving thought

The site should preserve the development of ideas over time.

It should make the work legible to others without pretending the work is more complete, certain, or authoritative than it is.

---

# Strategic position

Deep Field Works occupies a space between several familiar formats:

```text
research notebook → field journal → prototype lab → essay archive → concept library
```

It should not collapse fully into any one of these.

The site’s value comes from the combination:

* **field journal**: captures signals from the world
* **laboratory notebook**: documents experiments and prototypes
* **concept archive**: preserves reusable models and distinctions
* **publishing platform**: turns selected thinking into public artifacts
* **future seed bank**: stores ideas that may later become essays, videos, prototypes, or frameworks

Deep Field Works should feel like a serious, ongoing investigation.

Not a finished doctrine.

---

# Primary content goal

The primary content goal is to make emerging patterns easier to see.

A good Deep Field Works artifact should help clarify at least one of the following:

* what is changing
* what remains structurally unresolved
* what old framing no longer explains
* what new distinction is useful
* what human role is shifting
* what interface, workflow, or governance model may be needed
* what uncertainty should be watched over time

The goal is not to publish constantly.

The goal is to preserve and refine useful thinking.

# Strategic themes

Deep Field Works should focus on recurring themes rather than chase isolated news.

## Human-machine collaboration

How people think, decide, create, supervise, and work with increasingly capable systems.

Questions:

* What should humans still do directly?
* What should machines handle?
* What changes when AI becomes a collaborator rather than a tool?
* How does human judgment remain visible and accountable?

---

## Agentic workflows and governance

How autonomous or semi-autonomous systems should act within human-defined boundaries.

Questions:

* Who authorizes an agent to act?
* What actions can proceed without review?
* What requires escalation?
* How should confidence, reversibility, and risk shape automation?
* What audit trails are needed?

---

## Organizational redesign

How AI changes the routing of information, decisions, approvals, and accountability.

Questions:

* What happens to middle layers when information no longer has to travel through people?
* Which parts of management become less valuable?
* Which parts become more important?
* Does AI reduce coordination friction or create a new oversight burden?
* How do organizations redesign authority rather than simply automate tasks?

---

## Interfaces for supervision

How interfaces change when the user is no longer only performing tasks, but supervising systems.

Questions:

* What does a governance interface look like?
* How should uncertainty be shown?
* How should recommendations expose reasoning?
* How does a user adjust posture, thresholds, and policy?
* What should be visible at a glance?
* What should require deeper inspection?

---

## Personal knowledge and externalized cognition

How notes, archives, models, and AI systems become part of a thinking process.

Questions:

* When does a knowledge base become more than storage?
* How do accumulated notes generate new artifacts?
* What is the difference between memory, retrieval, and insight?
* How can a personal archive become a cognitive institution?

---

## Design practice in the AI era

How product design changes when AI can generate, interpret, summarize, prototype, and act.

Questions:

* What remains valuable about interaction design?
* What design artifacts become more important?
* What happens when static screens are no longer enough?
* How should designers represent behavior, timing, state, and governance?
* What does a useful proof of concept demonstrate?

# Content types

Deep Field Works should use a small set of stable content types.

Each type has a different purpose.

The goal is to make the archive easier for both humans and agents to understand.

---

## Note

A short capture of a thought, signal, distinction, or unresolved question.

Use when:

* the idea is interesting but early
* the signal should be preserved
* the thought may become useful later
* there is not yet enough material for an essay

A note should usually include:

* the observation or question
* why it may matter
* what remains unclear

A note does not need to resolve the idea.

---

## Field Report

A structured observation from a real-world signal.

Use when:

* responding to an article, announcement, product release, workplace pattern, or emerging behavior
* capturing something that may indicate a larger shift
* preserving early interpretation without overclaiming

A field report should usually include:

* the observed signal
* the possible interpretation
* why the signal matters
* what is not being said
* what to watch next

Field reports are especially useful for current developments that may later become durable patterns.

---

## Essay

A developed argument, model, or distinction.

Use when:

* the idea has enough shape to stand on its own
* the reader needs a clearer frame
* the piece can produce a durable takeaway
* the argument connects multiple observations

An essay should usually include:

* reader question or tension
* old framing that no longer works
* clearer model or claim
* concrete scenario
* implications
* unresolved questions
* sharp ending

Essays should be selective.

Not every idea needs to become one.

---

## Experiment

A record of something built, tested, simulated, or prototyped.

Use when:

* documenting a proof of concept
* explaining what an interface demonstrates
* preserving design decisions
* testing whether an abstract idea works in practice

An experiment should usually include:

* purpose
* what was built
* what it demonstrates
* what it does not solve
* what should be tested next

Experiments do not need production polish.

They need conceptual clarity.

---

## Prototype Note

A shorter companion to an experiment.

Use when:

* documenting a small design decision
* capturing an interaction pattern
* explaining a screen, flow, state, or behavior
* linking prototype behavior to a larger theme

A prototype note should usually include:

* the interaction or design choice
* what problem it addresses
* what larger pattern it illustrates
* what remains unresolved

Prototype notes can later roll up into a full experiment page.

---

## Concept Document

A durable explanation of a reusable framework, model, or distinction.

Use when:

* a concept appears across multiple pieces
* the same model will be referenced repeatedly
* agents need stable vocabulary
* the idea should become part of the shared knowledge layer

A concept document should usually include:

* concept name
* short definition
* why it matters
* where it applies
* related concepts
* examples
* open questions

Concept documents should be more stable than field reports, but still revisable.

---

## Checkpoint

A synthesis of accumulated thinking at a point in time.

Use when:

* several notes or essays are starting to form a larger pattern
* a project direction has changed
* an earlier assumption needs to be updated
* a body of work needs to be compressed for future retrieval

A checkpoint should usually include:

* what has accumulated
* what changed
* current working model
* open questions
* next directions

Checkpoints are important because Deep Field Works is chronological.

They help later readers and agents understand how the thinking evolved.

---

## Seed

A raw or lightly processed idea that may become something later.

Use when:

* the thought is too early even for a note
* the idea may connect to future work
* the purpose is preservation, not publication

A seed may include:

* a question
* a phrase
* a possible title
* a fragment
* a diagram
* a connection to another idea

Seeds do not need to be public by default.

They may live in an inbox or working folder until reviewed.

---

# Content lifecycle

Deep Field Works content should move through stages.

Not every item moves through every stage.

```text
Signal → Seed → Note → Field Report → Essay / Experiment → Concept Document → Checkpoint
```

## Signal

A signal is something noticed.

Examples:

* an article
* a product announcement
* a workplace pattern
* a recurring phrase
* a prototype problem
* a design decision
* a personal observation
* a shift in how AI tools are being used

A signal does not need interpretation yet.

---

## Seed

A seed preserves the signal or early thought.

It may be messy.

The only requirement is that future retrieval should be possible.

A good seed should include enough context to answer:

* What prompted this?
* Why did it seem interesting?
* What might it connect to?

---

## Note

A note adds minimal structure.

It turns a seed into something reusable.

A note should clarify:

* the observation
* the possible meaning
* the open question

---

## Field Report

A field report interprets a signal in context.

It should be careful about evidence.

It should distinguish what is visible from what is inferred.

---

## Essay or Experiment

An essay develops the idea into an argument.

An experiment tests or demonstrates the idea through a prototype, interface, workflow, or artifact.

Some ideas should become essays.

Some should become experiments.

Some should become both.

---

## Concept Document

A concept document extracts the durable model from several pieces.

It is less tied to one event.

It gives agents and humans stable vocabulary.

---

## Checkpoint

A checkpoint compresses accumulated thinking.

It is useful after a period of exploration, a major prototype, a thematic cluster, or a change in direction.

---

# Publication priorities

Deep Field Works should prioritize pieces that create durable value.

Priority should be given to content that:

* clarifies a recurring pattern
* introduces a reusable distinction
* connects AI capability to human or organizational redesign
* documents a prototype or experiment
* captures a shift in thinking
* helps future agents retrieve and extend the work
* remains useful after a specific news cycle fades

Lower priority should be given to content that:

* only reacts to news
* summarizes an article without adding a model
* repeats generic AI discourse
* depends too heavily on a company announcement
* exists mainly to sound current
* makes claims that are too broad to test
* is not connected to the larger investigation

---

# What belongs on Deep Field Works

A piece probably belongs on the site if it does one or more of the following:

* advances a line of inquiry
* documents a prototype or experiment
* captures a durable pattern
* sharpens a useful distinction
* shows how an abstract AI shift becomes operational
* connects human judgment, workflow, interface, or governance
* preserves a meaningful checkpoint in thinking
* can be referenced by future work

A piece may not belong if it is only:

* a generic opinion about AI
* a summary of someone else’s article
* a personal update without conceptual value
* a tool review without a larger pattern
* a trend reaction without durable framing
* a polished version of something that should remain a private note

---

# Relationship between public and private material

Not everything needs to be public.

Deep Field Works should have a distinction between:

```text
private working material → publishable artifact → durable reference
```

## Private working material

Includes:

* messy notes
* raw article reactions
* unfinished prompts
* personal context
* speculative fragments
* rough prototype notes
* discarded directions

These can remain in private folders or project context.

---

## Publishable artifact

Includes:

* field reports
* notes
* essays
* experiments
* prototype writeups
* checkpoints

These should be clear enough for an intelligent outside reader.

They do not need to be definitive.

---

## Durable reference

Includes:

* concept documents
* taxonomies
* frameworks
* source-of-truth docs
* recurring models
* article templates

These should be stable enough for future agents to retrieve and apply.

They can evolve, but changes should be deliberate.

---

## Three-layer evidence architecture

Research-heavy work should preserve a clear boundary between internal evidence, the public argument, and optional public references.

### Layer 1 — Internal research dossier

Approved location:

```text
docs/development/research/
```

Purpose:

* deep research
* source inventory
* access dates
* claim ledger
* counterevidence
* rejected or unsupported claims
* provenance
* editorial conclusions
* research questions

Research dossiers may be detailed. Retain them in Git when the source material is safe for repository access. They must remain production excluded and non-reader-facing. They must not contain credentials, sensitive data, or copyrighted full-text source material.

### Layer 2 — Public article

The public article is a short synthesis: a sharp pattern, distinction, or argument supported by only enough evidence to establish the point.

The article is not the research dossier.

### Layer 3 — Public references companion

A research-heavy public piece may have an optional references page belonging to the parent article.

Conceptual URL:

```text
<article-url>/references
```

Example:

```text
/field-notes/capacity-expansion-under-a-higher-standard-of-proof/references
```

Its purpose is a simple source list supporting the parent article.

Presentation should remain intentionally plain:

* source titles and links
* optional numbering that matches article citation markers
* no research narrative
* no claim ledger
* no explanation of what each source proves
* no independent editorial argument

The companion page:

* belongs to its parent article
* is not an independent content artifact
* is not listed in chronology
* is not listed on domain pages
* is not treated as a related piece
* is not independently canonical
* is not surfaced on homepage or archive listings
* inherits the parent publication lifecycle
* is reached through a restrained **Sources and references** link on the parent article

This architecture does not approve a schema, route, or UI implementation. Those require separate human review.

### Citation direction

Avoid long inline source-title links throughout public prose. Prefer restrained numbered citation markers or another Figma-approved lightweight treatment. Markers may resolve to the companion references page.

The exact visual and interaction behavior remains intentionally unresolved pending Figma design and human review.

# Site structure strategy

The site should support both chronological discovery and thematic discovery.

Chronology matters because the work is developmental.

Themes matter because readers and agents need entry points.

A useful structure may include:

```text
/
  index
  about
  field-notes
  essays
  experiments
  concepts
  checkpoints
  archive
```

## Home

The home page should orient the reader quickly.

It should answer:

* What is Deep Field Works?
* What kind of work lives here?
* What questions does it explore?
* Where should a new reader begin?

The home page should not over-explain.

It should create enough clarity for someone to enter the archive.

---

## About

The about page should explain the project’s purpose and stance.

It should clarify that Deep Field Works is:

* exploratory
* provisional
* independent
* not optimized for audience growth
* focused on human-machine work, governance, interfaces, and evolving systems

It should avoid sounding like a startup, consultancy, manifesto, or personal brand pitch.

---

## Field Notes

Field notes should preserve observations and early interpretations.

This section can be more chronological and active.

It is where current signals can be captured without needing a complete essay.

---

## Essays

Essays should contain the more developed public arguments.

This section should be selective.

An essay should provide a clear reader payoff.

---

## Experiments

Experiments should document prototypes, interface studies, and workflow models.

This section is important because Deep Field Works should not only comment on change.

It should build small demonstrations of possible futures.

---

## Concepts

Concepts should contain stable models, frameworks, and definitions.

This section should act as a shared vocabulary layer for both readers and agents.

---

## Checkpoints

Checkpoints should summarize how thinking has evolved.

They help preserve chronology without forcing every reader to reconstruct the entire archive.

---

## Archive

The archive should preserve publication order.

It allows readers to see the development of the work over time.

It also helps agents understand sequence, context, and conceptual evolution.

# Topic selection criteria

Before creating a new piece, ask:

1. What signal, question, or pattern prompted this?
2. Is this part of a recurring theme?
3. What would the reader understand better afterward?
4. Is this a note, field report, essay, experiment, concept document, or checkpoint?
5. Is the idea durable, timely, or both?
6. What evidence or example supports it?
7. What remains uncertain?
8. Does it connect to existing work?
9. Should it be public now, saved privately, or developed further?
10. Could this become future video, prototype, or concept material?

If the answer to most of these is unclear, the item may be a seed rather than a publishable piece.

Deep Field Works should allow clusters of related work to form over time.

A cluster may include:

* seeds
* notes
* field reports
* essays
* prototypes
* concept documents
* checkpoints

Example cluster:

```text
Agent authority
  → field report on autonomous trading
  → note on delegated authority
  → prototype for monitoring preferences
  → essay on governance interfaces
  → concept document on confidence-based automation
  → checkpoint on agent supervision
```

Clusters should not be over-planned too early.

Let repeated signals reveal which clusters matter.

# Relationship to prototypes

Prototypes are central to Deep Field Works.

They are not portfolio decoration.

They are small demonstrations of larger questions.

A prototype should usually connect to a content theme.

Examples:

* A monitoring preferences prototype demonstrates governance through user-adjustable thresholds.
* A decision surface prototype demonstrates interfaces for interpreting ambiguous signals.
* An airfare monitoring prototype demonstrates delegated autonomy under uncertainty.
* A design-system parser workflow demonstrates AI-assisted production infrastructure.

Prototype writeups should explain:

* what question the prototype explores
* what behavior it demonstrates
* what assumptions it makes
* what it leaves unresolved
* how it connects to larger themes

The prototype does not need to be technically complete.

It needs to make the concept tangible.

# Agent usage model

Agents should not treat every request as a request to publish.

They should first classify the task.

Recommended sequence:

```text
1. Read project-overview.md
2. Read content-strategy.md
3. Identify content type
4. Check related concepts
5. Use article-templates.md if drafting
6. Use editorial-guidelines.md when revising
7. Use voice-and-style.md for final pass
8. Update metadata or index if publishing
```

Agents should determine whether the input is:

* raw signal
* seed
* note
* field report
* essay
* experiment
* prototype note
* concept document
* checkpoint
* source-of-truth update

Agents should not automatically inflate a seed into an essay.

The right artifact is often smaller.

# Human review checkpoints

Human review is especially important when a piece:

* makes a strong claim
* discusses a current company or public event
* uses personal context
* defines a durable concept
* changes project direction
* updates a source-of-truth document
* becomes part of the public site
* could affect professional positioning

Agents may draft, compress, classify, and suggest.

Humans should approve publication and major conceptual changes.

# Archival strategy

Deep Field Works should preserve old thinking without pretending it was always correct.

When thinking changes:

* do not silently rewrite older pieces to match current views
* add updates where necessary
* create checkpoints to explain shifts
* mark superseded pieces when appropriate
* preserve publication dates
* link old pieces to newer interpretations

The archive should show development.

It should not simulate perfect foresight.

# Updating and revising content

Revisions should be handled differently depending on the type of change.

## Minor edits

Examples:

* typos
* formatting
* small wording improvements
* broken links

These can be updated directly.

## Clarifying edits

Examples:

* clearer explanation
* improved heading
* added example
* better distinction

These can be updated directly if they do not change the meaning.

## Substantive revisions

Examples:

* changed conclusion
* new evidence
* reversed interpretation
* significant reframing
* altered concept definition

These should usually be handled with:

* an update note
* a new checkpoint
* a superseding piece
* a linked revision history

Do not erase meaningful uncertainty or earlier error.

# Content strategy principles

## 1. Prefer durable patterns over temporary reactions

A current event matters when it reveals a pattern.

Do not chase every AI announcement.

Extract what may remain true after the announcement fades.

---

## 2. Keep the artifact size appropriate to the idea

Not every thought needs to become an essay.

Use the smallest artifact that preserves the value.

```text
fragment → seed
early question → note
external signal → field report
tested interface → experiment
reusable model → concept document
accumulated shift → checkpoint
developed argument → essay
```

---

## 3. Let clusters emerge before over-organizing

Do not force a complete taxonomy too early.

Repeated signals should reveal the structure.

Organize once there is enough material to justify the category.

---

## 4. Preserve uncertainty as part of the work

The site should show what was known, inferred, and unresolved at the time.

Uncertainty is not a weakness if it is specific.

---

## 5. Make abstract shifts operational

When possible, show what changes in:

* a workflow
* a role
* an interface
* a decision
* an approval process
* a review loop
* a governance model

Deep Field Works should avoid floating above practice.

---

## 6. Use prototypes as arguments

A prototype can make an argument without saying everything in prose.

When a prototype demonstrates a concept, the writeup should make the argument visible.

The prototype and article should reinforce each other.

---

## 7. Build a reusable concept layer

When an idea appears repeatedly, extract it into a concept document.

This helps:

* readers orient themselves
* agents retrieve the right context
* future pieces avoid redefining the same idea
* the archive become cumulative

---

## 8. Avoid audience capture

Do not let imagined audience demand distort the work.

The site may eventually attract readers, but it should not become driven by:

* engagement optimization
* trend-chasing
* hot takes
* personal branding
* forced regular publishing
* exaggerated certainty

The work should remain useful even if the audience is small.

---

## 9. Maintain professional optionality

Deep Field Works may become useful professionally, but it should not sound like a consulting pitch.

Avoid framing the site as:

* a startup
* a firm
* a service business
* a thought-leadership platform
* an influencer project

Better framing:

* independent research archive
* prototype lab
* field journal
* working library
* public notebook

The site should demonstrate judgment, curiosity, and capability without overselling.

---

## 10. Document the transition while participating in it

Deep Field Works is not only about AI-driven change.

It is also an example of AI-assisted thinking, writing, prototyping, synthesis, and publishing.

The process is part of the subject.

The archive should preserve both:

* what is being studied
* how the study is being conducted

# Common strategic mistakes

## Turning every signal into an essay

Most signals should become seeds, notes, or field reports first.

Essays should emerge from repeated patterns or unusually clear tensions.

---

## Overbuilding the taxonomy too early

A rigid structure can make the project feel more mature than it is.

Let the structure grow from the work.

---

## Publishing generic AI commentary

Avoid pieces that could appear on any AI blog.

Deep Field Works should be specific to its investigation: human-machine work, governance, interfaces, organizational redesign, prototypes, and externalized cognition.

---

## Losing chronology

If old ideas are constantly rewritten, the archive loses one of its main values.

Preserve the trail.

---

## Confusing polish with insight

A piece can sound finished without saying much.

Do not publish just because the prose is smooth.

---

## Confusing personal exploration with personal branding

The work can be personal without becoming self-promotional.

The site should reveal a way of thinking.

It should not perform expertise.

---

# Decision rules for agents

When an agent receives new material, it should decide what kind of action is appropriate.

## If the material is raw and unclear

Create or update a seed.

Do not overdevelop.

## If the material contains a clear observation

Create or update a note.

## If the material responds to an external event or article

Create a field report.

Preserve the distinction between observed facts and interpretation.

## If the material presents a developed argument

Draft or update an essay.

Use editorial guidelines for structure and revision.

## If the material describes a prototype

Create or update an experiment or prototype note.

Explain what the prototype demonstrates.

## If the material defines a reusable idea

Create or update a concept document.

Add related concepts where possible.

## If the material summarizes accumulated thinking

Create a checkpoint.

Preserve what changed and what remains open.

## If the material changes how the project should operate

Suggest a source-of-truth update.

Do not silently overwrite canonical documents without human review.

# Recommended publishing flow

A lightweight publishing flow:

```text
Inbox
  → classify
  → seed / note / field report / draft
  → human review
  → editorial pass
  → metadata check
  → publish
  → index / link related pieces
  → checkpoint when patterns accumulate
```

This process should stay lightweight.

The goal is not to create bureaucracy.

The goal is to prevent useful thinking from being lost and prevent weak drafts from being published too easily.

# Minimum viable publication standard

Before something is published, it should answer:

1. What is this piece?
2. Why does it exist?
3. What does it help clarify?
4. What evidence, example, or observation anchors it?
5. What remains uncertain?
6. How does it connect to the larger Deep Field Works investigation?

If those questions cannot be answered, the piece should remain a seed or draft.

---

# Final strategy principle

Deep Field Works should become a cumulative thinking system.

Each artifact should either:

* preserve a signal
* clarify a question
* test a model
* document an experiment
* refine a concept
* connect ideas
* mark a change in understanding

The site does not need to publish often.

It needs to make the investigation more durable, navigable, and useful over time.
