# Context inequality

A person who can define good output, provide examples, explain tradeoffs, clarify constraints, and identify failure modes will get much more from AI than someone who simply knows which chatbot to open. The model can generate options, but the human still needs to know what “better” means.

## Why it may matter

AI may raise the floor without raising the ceiling evenly. Broad model access can still produce unequal leverage when some people have more usable context or can articulate judgment more clearly than others.

## Current interpretation

This is a provisional hypothesis, not an observed hierarchy. The next AI divide may depend less on access to a powerful model than on access to usable context and the ability to express judgment clearly enough for machines to assist it.

“Learn AI tools” is useful advice, but incomplete. Tool fluency matters. The deeper advantage may come from making judgment, preferences, examples, tradeoffs, and constraints explicit enough for an AI system to use. The relevant contrast is not simply between people who use AI and people who do not. It is between generic access and context-rich access; between asking for outputs and making judgment systems legible.

“AI social strata” is a working lens for that possibility, not a claim that a stable class system already exists.

## Concrete examples

A designer who can explain why one interaction works better than another can use AI to generate, compare, critique, and refine. A designer with only vague preferences gets plausible sameness.

A manager who can define escalation thresholds can use AI to monitor and surface exceptions. A manager who cannot express the difference between “normal drift” and “meaningful risk” gets noise.

## Open question

If model access becomes broadly shared, which forms of context and judgment will still create unequal leverage?
